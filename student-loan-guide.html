Student Loans: Understand Repayment Choices

Learn how U.S. federal and private student loans differ, how interest grows, and what to check before changing your repayment plan.

Last reviewed: November 24, 2026

1. Start by identifying every loan

Make a separate record for each loan or loan group. Note the lender or federal servicer, current principal, accrued unpaid interest, interest rate, whether the rate is fixed or variable, required payment, due date, and repayment status. For federal loans, sign in to StudentAid.gov and check the Dashboard for loan type, balance, servicer and first-disbursement information. For private loans, use the lender's current statement and your promissory note.

Do not assume that loans from the same school have identical rates or repayment rights. Federal Direct, older FFEL, Parent PLUS and private loans can have different rules. Keep federal and private balances separate when comparing options.

2. Understand how student-loan interest works

Interest is the cost of borrowing and is generally calculated against the unpaid principal. Federal Direct Loans accrue interest daily. A useful daily-interest approximation is:

Daily interest ≈ outstanding principal × (annual interest rate ÷ 365.25)

The actual amount depends on the balance and number of days between payments. A monthly estimate using APR divided by 12 is convenient for a simple calculator, but it will not exactly reproduce a servicer's daily calculation.

Unpaid interest may accumulate during certain periods, including some income-driven repayment arrangements, forbearance, or deferment on unsubsidized loans. If interest capitalizes, it is added to principal; future interest can then be calculated on the larger balance. Capitalization rules depend on loan type and circumstances, so check the current official explanation rather than assuming every unpaid-interest amount immediately becomes principal.

Federal Direct Subsidized and Unsubsidized Loans also treat in-school and deferment interest differently. For eligible subsidized loans, the Department of Education pays specified interest during qualifying periods; borrowers are generally responsible for interest on unsubsidized loans during those periods.

3. Federal repayment rules changed in 2026

Federal student-loan repayment options now depend in part on the type of loan and when it was first disbursed. The July 1, 2026 date is an important dividing line in current Federal Student Aid guidance. For example, borrowers with loans first disbursed on or after that date may have a different set of fixed and income-driven plan choices from borrowers whose loans were disbursed earlier. Mixed loan histories can make eligibility more complicated.

Federal Student Aid's current guidance describes the Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) and explains how eligibility for income-driven repayment options depends on loan type and disbursement date. The SAVE Plan is no longer available following a federal court order. Do not rely on an old article, a plan name remembered from a previous year, or a generic calculator to decide eligibility. Review the live Federal Student Aid repayment-plan information and the current income-driven repayment FAQs.

Before switching plans, compare the monthly payment, repayment period, total expected amount paid, interest treatment, forgiveness conditions, and any annual income or family-size recertification requirement. A lower required payment does not automatically mean a lower total cost.

4. Compare federal and private loans carefully

Federal loans may provide access to federal repayment plans and protections such as eligible deferment, forbearance, discharge or forgiveness programs. Private loans are governed by the lender's contract and applicable law; they generally do not carry the same federal program protections. Private rates may be fixed or variable, and a variable rate can change over time.

Refinancing federal loans into a private loan can sometimes offer a lower quoted rate, but it permanently replaces the federal loan with private debt. You can lose access to federal repayment plans and federal borrower protections. Compare the full terms—not just the advertised monthly payment or starting rate—and understand what protections you would give up before signing.

5. Make extra payments deliberately

If your required payments are current and your budget allows extra repayment, ask your servicer how to direct the additional amount to principal or to a particular loan group. Check how the servicer handles paid-ahead status, accrued interest and multiple loan groups. Keep making at least the required payment unless your servicer confirms a different arrangement in writing.

When choosing which loan to pay extra, compare interest rates, balances, variable-rate risk and any special loan benefits. Paying the highest-rate balance first can reduce interest in a simplified comparison, while paying a small balance first may help some people stay motivated. Keep an emergency cash buffer and avoid using high-cost credit to make student-loan payments.

6. If payments become difficult

Contact your federal servicer or private lender before missing payments where possible. Ask which options apply to your specific loan and what each option means for interest, credit reporting, fees and the time needed to repay. Do not stop paying solely because you submitted an application or read that a program may apply to you; confirm the status with the servicer.

Be cautious of companies that charge fees for help that the federal government provides for free, guarantee loan forgiveness, or ask for your StudentAid.gov password or one-time security code. Use official government websites and contact information from your account.

7. A practical review checklist

  • List every loan separately and confirm whether it is federal or private.
  • Check the current principal, accrued interest, rate, payment and repayment status.
  • For federal loans, verify loan type and first disbursement date in StudentAid.gov.
  • Compare only repayment plans for which your actual loans are eligible.
  • Estimate both monthly cash flow and total repayment cost.
  • Before refinancing, write down the federal protections and options you would lose.
  • Save current statements and confirmation messages after any repayment-plan change.
